Coverly cover study: three color treatments

One cover composition, set three ways. Typography, artwork, layout and copy are identical; only color changes. All artists, songs and stories are fictional.

Open any version full screen, then press 1, 2 or 3 to flip between the palettes in place, which is the fairest way to compare them. Move the pointer over the cover to see the layers separate, hover the wordmark letters to see their B-side, and hover “Explore the six versions” to fan out the echoes.

1. Electric Pop

Printer’s ink with pink, electric blue and turquoise. Loud, nocturnal, screen-native.

Strong for

  • The highest energy and contrast of the three; saturated hues stay pure on the dark base.
  • Feels like a night out, a club flyer, a screen at 2am.

Watch out for

  • Long-form reading on a dark background is tiring; articles would need a lighter reading surface.
  • Closest to the dark music-app look you wanted to avoid; it relies on strong artwork to escape it.

2. Retro Remix

Warm paper with orange-red, cobalt and violet, printed like a two-color riso.

Strong for

  • The most “printed magazine” of the three: red and cobalt on paper is the pairing your references use most.
  • The calmest base for long-form reading, so the magazine and the exploration pages can share it.

Watch out for

  • Paper backgrounds slide toward vintage if the type or artwork gets nostalgic.
  • Red and cobalt are common in poster design; the artwork system has to carry the distinctiveness.
  • Small red text needs a darker red (#C42E12) to stay readable; the bright red is for artwork only.

3. After Hours

Deep plum with coral, purple and turquoise. Warm, intimate, late.

Strong for

  • The most distinctive base; coral against turquoise gives energy with warmth instead of glare.
  • Suits listening, intimacy and music after dark.

Watch out for

  • Plum is a strong flavor across a whole site; long-form would need a paper reading surface.
  • Purple-heavy identities can read as nightlife only, narrower than a general music magazine.

Whichever base you choose becomes the magazine’s constant. Individual stories can still swap in their own ink pair, the way every poster on your board does, so the system stays varied without losing its identity.

Typography

Three voices with clearly separate jobs. All are released under the SIL Open Font License and served by Google Fonts, so they are free to use in the product.

Bagel Fat One

The B-side. Lettering only: the flipped wordmark, the orbit line, notes. Never body text.

every song has another side
Bricolage Grotesque

The A-side. Wordmark (condensed, heavy, the only uppercase) and sentence-case headlines; width and weight vary by role.

Schibsted Grotesk

The reading voice. Navigation, summaries, metadata, buttons and long-form articles. Built for a news publisher, with italics.

How the artwork is built

All artwork is original CSS and SVG, with no photography, so the cover works for songs that have no artist images. From back to front:

  1. Rays converge on the subject’s head and fade out before the text column.
  2. Echoes: five offset silhouettes, one per cover version, stepping back toward the background color. The artwork encodes the story: one original, five covers.
  3. Wordmark: in front of the echoes, behind the figure, so the cover star steps out of the logo.
  4. The far half of the orbit, hidden behind the head.
  5. The figure: a listener in profile, shaded with a real 45° halftone screen lit from the front, with a paper cut-out edge.
  6. The near half of the orbit carries the line “every song has another side” in front of the neck, and a small paper satellite travels the full loop.
  7. Editorial text on a calm, gridded column, then grain over everything so ink and paper read as printed.

Motion

  • One entrance: wordmark letters flip from their bubble B-side to the A-side, the echoes slide out from behind the head, and the story text rises.
  • Ambient: only the satellite moves, slowly.
  • Responsive: the pointer separates the layers by depth; letters flip on hover; hovering the main button fans the echoes and speeds up the satellite.
  • With reduced motion turned on, the cover is shown in its final, still state.

Not designed yet

  • Small screens currently use a provisional stacked layout. The deliberately designed mobile cover comes in checkpoint 2, along with editorial cards and the original/cover system.
